Dambulla Cave Temple Guide

Five rock chambers cut into a granite outcrop above Dambulla town, holding rows of Buddha statues under painted ceilings. Here is what it costs, what the climb involves, what the dress code actually means in practice, and what is inside each cave.

At a glance

Opening hours
About 7:00 to 19:00, but the ticket counter closes at 17:00
Ticket price
About LKR 2,000 for a foreign visitor, plus about LKR 100 for shoe storage
Time needed
One and a half to two hours, including the climb
Best time to visit
Early morning, or late afternoon before the counter shuts
Getting there
About 17 km from Sigiriya, on the Kandy to Trincomalee road
Steps to the caves
Around 350, on a paved and moderately steep path

The short version

Why there are temples inside a rock

The caves were shelter before they were a temple. Tradition holds that a king who had lost his throne hid here among the monks, and endowed the site once he had taken it back. From then on successive rulers added statues, plaster, paint and finally the drip ledges and walls that turned open rock overhangs into enclosed shrine rooms.

The result is not one building but an accumulation: roughly two thousand years of commissions layered into five chambers, repainted and extended by people who were not trying to preserve a monument so much as keep a working temple in use. It is still a working temple today, which is why the dress code and the barefoot rule are enforced rather than suggested. UNESCO inscribed it as a World Heritage Site.

Inside

The five caves

They run in a row along the rock face, each behind its own doorway off a shared whitewashed verandah. You visit them in sequence.

  • Cave oneThe smallest, and the one most people photograph: a reclining Buddha cut from the living rock, running most of the length of the chamber.
  • Cave twoThe largest and the best of them. Dozens of statues around the walls, and a painted ceiling that follows the curve of the rock overhead.
  • Cave threeLater work, and it looks it: the painting is more confident and the figures more crowded.
  • Caves four and fiveSmaller, quieter, and usually empty of other visitors. Worth the extra few minutes.
  • The ceilingsThe thing people remember. The paint follows the natural rock rather than a flat plane, so the patterns bend overhead.
  • The view backFrom the terrace outside the caves you can see across the plain, with Sigiriya rock visible on a clear day.

Step by step

How to visit

  1. Check the counter time, not the site timeThe caves stay open later than the ticket office. Arriving at half past five means you will be turned away.
  2. Dress before you arriveShoulders and knees covered, for everyone. A scarf is not a workaround if you are in shorts.
  3. Start up the pathAround 350 steps, paved, steady rather than steep. Twenty to thirty minutes at an easy pace.
  4. Leave your shoes at the topThere is a shoe minder near the entrance for a small charge. From here you are barefoot.
  5. Work along the verandahCaves one to five in order. Cave two is the one to slow down in.
  6. Mind the monkeysThey are used to people and will take food or anything loose straight out of your hand.
  7. Come back down the same wayThere is one path. Stone steps, barefoot feet and midday heat are a bad combination, so put your shoes back on.

Before you go

Heat, dress code and what to bring

What to bring

A refillable water bottle. Clothing that covers shoulders and knees, or a sarong you can wrap over what you are wearing. Socks, if you would rather not cross hot stone barefoot. Cash for the ticket and the shoe minder.

Heat and crowds

The climb is open to the sun and the terrace outside the caves has little shade. Inside, the rock keeps the chambers noticeably cooler, which is part of the pleasure of the place. Coach groups tend to come through late morning, so early is both cooler and quieter.

Accessibility

There is no step-free route. Reaching the caves means around 350 paved steps with handrails in places, and the chambers themselves have raised thresholds you step over. Anyone who cannot manage sustained stairs will not be able to reach the caves, and there is no alternative access.

Travel plastic-free

The stalls at the bottom of the path sell a lot of bottled water to people about to climb.

  • Fill a refillable bottle before you start rather than buying at the gate.
  • Bring a cloth bag for anything you pick up from the stalls.
  • Carry your rubbish back down. This is a religious site and a World Heritage one.

ZeroPlastic Impact Center

On the road between Dambulla and Sigiriya

If you are carrying on from Dambulla towards Sigiriya, the ZeroPlastic Impact Center is on the main road, about 5 km before Sigiriya Rock. It is on the way rather than a detour, and it is free to walk into.

Local artisans work at an open bench, and visitors can join hands-on sessions: carving a coconut shell, painting a traditional mask, or watching recovered bottle lids turned into small objects. The centre works with 249 artisan families and carries more than 1,000 plastic-free products. ZeroPlastic Movement is a registered Sri Lankan non-profit, and the point of the place is the mission rather than the shop.

Entry is free, with no ticket and no booking for a normal visit, and most people spend twenty to thirty minutes. Water refills and washrooms are free to travellers. Hands-on workshops are paid and arranged separately.

Questions

Sigiriya Lion Rock FAQ

How much is the Dambulla Cave Temple entrance fee?

The figure quoted almost everywhere is LKR 2,000 for a foreign visitor, with a small separate charge of about LKR 100 for shoe storage at the top. Prices are not published on an official page that we could find, so treat this as indicative and check at the counter.

What are the opening hours?

The site is generally open 7:00 to 19:00, but the ticket counter closes at 17:00, so arriving after five means you will not get in. Aim to buy your ticket well before that.

How many steps are there, and how hard is the climb?

Around 350 steps on a moderately steep paved path, usually 20 to 30 minutes at an easy pace. It is a steady walk rather than a scramble, but it is exposed and there is little shade.

What is the dress code?

Shoulders and knees covered, for everyone. This is a working Buddhist temple, not only a monument. Shoes and hats come off before you enter the caves, so you will be walking barefoot on stone that gets hot in the middle of the day.

Is Dambulla worth it if I have already seen Sigiriya?

They are different things. Sigiriya is an archaeological site you climb for the ruins and the view. Dambulla is an interior: painted ceilings and rows of statues in cool, dim rock chambers. Most people doing the Cultural Triangle see both, often on the same day.

Can I take photographs inside the caves?

Photography is generally allowed, but turning your back on a Buddha image to pose for a photo causes real offence in Sri Lanka and has got visitors into serious trouble. Stand beside the statues, not in front of them.
